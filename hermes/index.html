Founding five

Walk-in script

  1. Opener. “I'm Tristan. Bryce and I run a little web studio here. Is the owner around?”
  2. Hook. “When you change a price or sell out at the register, does your website keep up?” Then listen.
  3. Ask what they want. “Do you want more orders, or fewer phone calls and your menu and hours showing right when people search?” Pitch to their answer.
  4. Offer. “We build sites that run from your Square, Shopify or Clover. We're taking five founding shops: your price is locked at $50 a month for two years, and we'll photograph your food or shop for free. All we ask is an honest review.”
  5. Show Bunny World on your phone, then close. “Want me to put you down as one of the five?” Book a slow time to set it up, then log it.

Never push past a no. A first visit can just be a heads-up.

If they say…

“No time.”
“That's the point. We do the work, and the hookup takes two minutes.”
“I have Facebook.”
“Keep it. This is what shows up when people search.”
“Don't need more business.”
“Then we keep it simple: menu and hours, or ordering you switch off when busy.”
“Nervous about my register.”
“You tap Connect on Square's own page, with no password shared. Change your mind and you owe nothing.”
“Too expensive.”
“$1,200 is about $23 a week for a year, roughly what a couple of online orders bring in. Or start at $50 a month.”
“What's the catch?”
“None. $50 a month from day one, cancel any time.”
“Not right now.”
“Fair. Can I leave a card and check back next week?”

Unsure? Leave a card and note when the manager's in.

What to say at the counter

Short, spoken, and in this order. Ask once, then stop talking.

Open · a heads-up, no ask

“Hi, I'm Tristan, I build websites here in Berthoud. I was checking local business sites on my phone and noticed something on yours. Can I show you? It takes thirty seconds.”

Under 20 seconds. If it's an employee: “Could you pass this to the owner? It's about a problem with the website.” Ask when the owner's usually in.

Show · their own site on your phone

“That's your site on a phone, last night.”

Hand over the printout face up, or turn your phone round. Point at the problem and say one sentence in their words:

  • Security warning: “Before anyone sees your site, the browser tells them it isn't safe.”
  • Someone else's page: “When people type your address, this comes up instead of you.”
  • Error or dead page: “This is what people get when they tap your website on Google.”
  • Phone layout: “On a phone, this is what people see. It's hard to read.”
Ask · what would it cost you to do it yourself?

“Can I ask you something? If you fixed this yourself, what do you think it would cost?”

Let them answer. Then be straight with them:

“Honestly, the builders are cheap: about $200 to $350 a year. What it really costs is your time. Most owners spend twenty to forty hours getting a site right, and then it's on you when something breaks. That's the part I take off your plate.”

If they want numbers, open the Costs tab and let them move the slider.

Price · the $250 fix first

“I can fix this one problem for $250, flat. The price is agreed before I start, and you pay once it's done and you've checked it.”

Say the one price on their page. Only if they ask what a whole new site costs: “$1,200 for a starter site, or $100 a month for twelve months, same total, no interest. A bigger site is $2,000.”

Trade · while we're getting established

“We're new and building our portfolio. If it helps, we're open to a trade for the first part, say gift cards, meals, a service you offer, or a review and a photo we can use.”

A trade can stand in for the first payment: the $250 fix, or the deposit or first months on a site.

  • Only Tristan or Bryce offer it; friends selling for commission never do.
  • Put the trade in writing on the work agreement, with what each side gives, its dollar value and when it happens.
  • The rest of the price stays cash at launch. Care is always cash.
  • A trade counts as income at its fair value for taxes (IRS rule on bartering), so log its value.
Demo · only if they have two minutes

“If you've got two minutes, I'll show you what one like yours could look like.”

Open the matching Bunny World shop on the laptop beside their site on the phone. “Here's yours today; here's what one like it could be.” Say it plainly: these are demo shops we built, not clients. Each stop card links its closest match.

Leave · the page, and the choice

“No pressure at all, you're free to say no. The page is yours to keep either way. Would you like me to take care of it?”

Ask once, then stop talking. Whatever they say, thank them and log it before the next stop.

When they say…

“My nephew does it.” / “We have someone.”

“Great, then hand them this page. It shows exactly what's wrong. If they ever get busy, I'm local and happy to help.”

“Too expensive.”

“That's fair. It's a flat $250 and nothing until it's fixed. If it's not worth that to you right now, that's completely fine.” For a site: “There's also $100 a month for twelve months. Same $1,200 total, no interest.”

“We're fine with Facebook.”

“Facebook's good for news, keep it. The trouble is that Google still sends people to your web address, and right now that's what they see. I can make that address show your hours, menu and a link to your Facebook.”

“Send me something.” / “Email me.”

“Happy to. What's the best address?” Write it down. Send one short note that day with the same page attached, and nothing more until they reply.

“Is this a scam?”

“Fair question, I'd ask too. I'm local. You don't pay anything until it's fixed and you've seen it working, and the price is in writing before I start.”

“Do you have references?”

“Not yet. I'm just starting out locally, so I'd rather be honest about it. Here's what I've built.” Show Bunny World and let them tap a shop. “That's why the first job is small and paid at the end.”

“We use Wix / Square / Squarespace.”

“Good, keep it. I'd fix it where it is, or move it, whichever costs you less.”

Rules
  • No guarantees: never “more customers”, “top of Google” or “you're losing $X”.
  • Never say “hacked”. Say “it's showing the wrong page” or “browsers show a warning”.
  • No discounts. The prices are the prices.
  • Never knock their current web person. Never invent clients: Bunny World is a demo.
  • One visit, one ask. Leave at once on “not interested” or if they're annoyed.

Before you walk in with the laptop

  • Their broken site open on your phone (or the printout's screenshot), ready to hand over.
  • The matching Bunny World shop open on the laptop, hotspot on, page already loaded.
  • The work agreement open in a second tab, so a yes can be written down on the spot.
  • This page on your phone, on their stop card, so “Log visit” is one tap on the way out.

Website owner’s guide

Your new website: how to get people using it

Your website now sends orders straight into your Square register. That only helps if people know it’s there, so here’s what actually works, in order of how much it helps for the time it takes. Most of it is ten minutes, once.

One thing worth knowing first: most people find a place like yours by word of mouth or by walking past, not by searching. So half of this guide is about Google, and half is about your counter, your bags and your window.

The first week (about two hours in all)

  1. Put your order page on Google, and make it the first choice. This is the biggest one.
    • Open your Google Business Profile (the box that shows when someone searches your name).
    • Add your website, [website].
    • Under Menu, add [website]/menu.
    • Under Food ordering, tap Add a link, paste [website]/order, then choose Set as preferred for pickup.
    • If delivery apps you don’t use show up in that list, turn off Show provider to customers for each one.
    • Check your hours while you’re there.

If you skip the "preferred" step, an app that takes up to 30% of every order can sit in that spot instead of you.

  1. Add Sea of Grass as a manager, not your password. If you’d like us to help with Google, go to People and access and invite [hello@seaofgrass.studio] as a Manager. You stay the owner, and you can remove us any time.
  2. Ask everyone for a Google review. We’ll print you a small card with a QR code that opens your review page. Hand one out with orders, or keep a stack by the register.
    • Ask everyone, not just the happy ones.
    • Never offer a discount or a freebie for a review. Google and the FTC both forbid it.
    • Don’t have staff or family review you.
    • Reviews matter more than almost anything: one extra star has been shown to bring independent restaurants 5–9% more sales, and about half of people won’t try a place with fewer than 20 reviews.
  3. Say yes to Apple Maps and Bing too. They’re free and take about 45 minutes together.
    • Every iPhone uses Apple Maps when someone asks Siri. Claim your place at business.apple.com.
    • Bing Places can copy your Google profile in a couple of clicks, and Bing feeds some of the AI search answers people now use.
  4. Put the website on your Square receipts. In your Square Dashboard, go to Settings > Account & Settings > Payments > Receipts, and type "Order ahead at [website]" in the extra text. Then send yourself a test receipt to check it shows.
  5. Update your bios. On Instagram and Facebook, put [website]/order in the link spot and write "Order ahead" next to it. On Yelp, claim your free listing and fix the hours and website. Please don’t ask anyone for Yelp reviews, because Yelp punishes it.
  6. Tell the people who already love you, once. Post one photo: "You can now order ahead at [website]. It’ll be ready when you walk in." Text it to regulars who’ve told you it’s fine to text them. One message is plenty.

At the counter (it keeps working every day)

  1. A QR card by the register and a cling on the window. We’ll give you both: "Skip the line next time: order ahead," with the code.
    • The QR is for next time. Keep your paper menu, because plenty of people don’t like ordering from a code while they’re standing there.
    • The window cling catches people walking past, which is how almost a third of people find somewhere new.
  2. Say it out loud. When someone’s waiting: "Next time you can order ahead on our website, and it’ll be ready when you walk in." One sentence, every day, and it sticks.
  3. Put it on bags, boxes and menus the next time you reorder them. Just the web address, in big letters.
  4. If you’re on DoorDash or Uber Eats, keep them. They help new people find you. But when a regular orders through them, tell them they can order direct next time. The apps take 15–30% of each delivery order; ordering through your own site costs you about 3%.

Keep it true (five minutes a week)

  1. Keep your hours the same everywhere : on Google, on Apple Maps and on your site. Wrong hours lose more customers than anything. Google also reads your website, so if the site is out of date, Google can change your correct hours to the wrong ones. Tell us about holiday hours and we’ll update the site; that’s part of your plan.
  2. Keep your menu and prices in Square. Your website reads from Square, so change things there and the site follows. Mark an item sold out in Square when you run out.
  3. Answer reviews , the good ones and the bad ones. A short, kind reply tells new customers there’s a real person here who cares.
  4. Leave your name, address and category on Google alone once they’re verified. Changing them can make Google ask you to verify all over again.

Once a month (fifteen minutes)

  1. Look at what sold online. In Square, go to Reports > Sales summary and group it by Source. If one item sells better online than in the shop, put it first on your site and on your counter card.
  2. Peek at Google. Your profile’s Performance page shows how many people called, tapped your website or asked for directions. Count your orders in Square, though; Google’s order count won’t include orders from your website.
  3. Post one real photo of something fresh, with your link. A phone photo of today’s special beats anything polished.

Things you can skip

  • Google "Posts" barely move anything. Do them only if you enjoy it.
  • Paying for listings on dozens of directories. Google, Apple, Bing and Yelp are the ones that matter.
  • Square Marketing and Square Loyalty come with Square’s Plus plan, $49 a month per location. They’re good tools if you’re already on Plus. If you’re not, start with everything above first.

When to call us

  • Something on the site looks wrong or an order didn’t come through: email hello@seaofgrass.studio, or call or text the number on your agreement. Care is part of your plan.
  • You want to add something: catering, gift cards, a new page. Just ask.

You run the shop. We keep the website working for you.
